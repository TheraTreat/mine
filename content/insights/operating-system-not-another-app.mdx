Ask any therapist in India to show you their practice's information system and they'll show you three things: a shelf of paper files, a WhatsApp thread, and their own memory. That's the stack. It runs most of the country's rehabilitation care.

Into this walks a steady parade of apps. Booking apps, clinic apps, exercise apps, EMRs built for hospitals and shrunk to fit. Most of them die in the same place: week three, when the therapist realises the app is one more thing to feed after the last patient leaves.

Here's the distinction I keep coming back to: **an app asks the clinician to do more work. An operating system removes work that already exists.**

## The work that already exists

An occupational therapist running a full OPD does the same invisible shift every day after the visible one ends:

- Session notes — written late, from memory, by hand
- Referral summaries — retyped into WhatsApp, losing structure on the way
- Home programmes — explained verbally, forgotten by Thursday
- Outcomes — measured inconsistently, recorded nowhere, comparable to nothing

None of this is optional. It's the legal, clinical and moral substrate of care. It's also 45–60 unpaid minutes a day, and it's why records in Indian rehab die in drawers while the patient moves on to the next clinician with a blank slate.

An app that adds a dashboard on top of this has misread the problem. The problem isn't that therapists lack software to use. It's that the work has no infrastructure to land on.

## Why the note is the wedge

When we started TheraTreat, the tempting first product was the visible one — scheduling, payments, a clinic front-desk in an app. We built toward the note instead, and I'd make the same call again, for one reason:

> The SOAP note is the atomic unit of clinical truth. Everything else — referrals, outcomes, billing, research — is derived from it. If the note is missing or unstructured, nothing downstream can be trusted.

So the wedge is TheraDocs: the therapist speaks the session — in Marathi, Hindi or English, code-switching allowed, because that's how Indian clinicians actually talk — and reviews a structured SOAP draft. Then they sign it. That last step is not a formality. The model drafts; the clinician signs. If an AI system in healthcare can't tell you whose name stands under its output, it isn't ready for a clinic.

## What "operating system" actually commits us to

Calling something an OS is a claim about gravity, not a feature list. It means:

1. **One record, not five silos.** Intake, notes, outcomes and billing reference the same patient object, so a referral is a permission change — not a retyped summary.
2. **Language as a first-class citizen.** A system Indian therapy runs on cannot be English-first with translation bolted on.
3. **Outcomes as a by-product.** If measuring progress requires extra effort, it won't happen. Structured notes make outcome data fall out of ordinary work.
4. **The clinician stays the author.** Automation that erodes accountability isn't efficiency. It's risk with better UX.

India doesn't need its therapists to become data-entry clerks for another app. It needs the work they already do to start compounding. That's the whole company in one sentence — and it's why we started with the most boring, most important artefact in the building: the note.
